Your career pathway

From job seeker to Registered Manager

Build experience, seek feedback and check the requirements for each role.

It starts in your own bedroom, looking at care vacancies and wondering where you might fit. Over time, the responsibility changes: first you learn to support children well, then you learn to lead a shift, develop a team and oversee the care provided by the whole home.

The flow chart shows one possible route through children’s residential care in England. Use each stage to work out what to learn, what responsibility to ask for and what evidence will show you’re ready for the next step.

1Job seeker → Support Worker: Show what you bring

Read the job description and find out who the home supports. Think about the everyday work: helping children with routines, building relationships, working shifts and staying calm when plans change. Ask what induction and support you’ll receive, especially if this is your first role in care.

You can draw on experience from work, volunteering, education or everyday life. Prepare examples that show how you listened to someone, kept a commitment, handled a disagreement or asked for help when something was beyond your experience. At interview, explain what you noticed, what you did and what you learned. Complete the application carefully, explain any gaps in your employment history and respond promptly to requests for recruitment checks.

What readiness looks like: You can explain why you want to work with children, give examples of how you build trust and show that you’re willing to learn. If you don’t know how to handle a situation, you can explain how you’d get help.

2Support Worker → Senior Support Worker: Become someone others can rely on

Start with the quality of your own practice. Learn how each child communicates, what helps them feel safe and what their plans ask you to do. Record clearly, raise concerns promptly and follow through on agreed actions. Use supervision to discuss what went well and what you’d do differently. If you don’t already hold the relevant Level 3 qualification, agree a plan with your employer. For staff in a care role, Regulation 32 normally requires the Level 3 Diploma for Residential Childcare (England), or an equivalent qualification, within two years of starting a care role in a home, subject to the permitted deferrals (Children’s Homes (England) Regulations 2015, reg 32).

What to ask for: Lead part of a handover with an experienced colleague alongside you. Practise explaining the priorities, who will follow up and what the next shift needs to know. Then ask for specific feedback. As you gain confidence, help a new colleague learn an agreed routine and check they understand why it matters.

What readiness looks like: You can give an example of keeping care consistent across shifts, helping a colleague improve their practice or spotting a concern and following it through. You can do this on a difficult shift as well as on a straightforward one.

3Senior Support Worker → Deputy Manager: Move from running a shift to improving the home

As a senior, you coordinate the shift in front of you. As a deputy, you also need to spot patterns across shifts and help the team improve. Build experience in staff supervision, induction, rota planning, incident review and checking the quality of records and care plans. Ask your manager to agree which decisions you can make and which you should escalate.

What to ask for: Take responsibility for one defined improvement. For example, if important actions are repeatedly missed at handover, review a sample of records, speak with the team and agree a simple change. Check a few weeks later whether actions are now being completed and what difference that made for children.

What readiness looks like: You can explain a problem you identified, how you involved staff, what you changed and how you checked it worked. You can also give an example of addressing poor practice fairly, setting clear expectations and following up.

4Deputy Manager → Registered Manager: Show you can lead the whole service

Build experience beyond covering for the manager. Get involved in safer recruitment, placement matching, safeguarding oversight, workforce planning and quality assurance. Work with the manager and Responsible Individual to understand how the home knows children are safe, making progress and being heard. Be ready to discuss what you would do when those checks show something is going wrong.

What to ask for: Lead a review with an agreed scope. Bring together children’s feedback, staff observations and records. Identify what needs to change, assign responsibilities and check whether the changes improved children’s experience. Use examples like this to prepare for the role and the Ofsted application.

Check the requirements early. Under Regulation 28, you need at least two years’ experience in a role relevant to the residential care of children within the previous five years, and at least one year in a role requiring you to supervise and manage staff working in care roles. Check your actual duties and dates against those requirements. The Level 5 Diploma in Leadership and Management for Residential Childcare (England), or a qualification the registered provider considers equivalent, must normally be gained within three years of starting to manage a home, subject to permitted deferral. The proposed manager must also apply to Ofsted through the SC2 process.

Make your next step specific

Ask your manager or mentor to agree one responsibility, what good practice looks like and when you’ll review it together. For example: “Over the next month, I’ll lead three handovers with feedback from a senior. At our next supervision, we’ll check whether I made the priorities clear and whether agreed actions were followed up.”

Keep a short record of what you did, the feedback you received and what improved. Leave out children’s identifying details. Those examples will help you explain at your next interview what you’re ready to take on and where you still need support.

Care leadership

The Psychological Profile of a Leader

You often see leadership most clearly when things don’t go to plan. In a care setting, that might be a shift when someone is distressed, staff disagree about what to do, and there’s pressure to act quickly. A good leader notices how their own stress is affecting them, listens to what others are seeing and decides what needs attention first. They can act decisively while staying open to the possibility that they’ve missed something.

Good leaders also make it easier for people to speak up. They pay attention to the colleague who rarely challenges a decision and the person receiving care whose wishes may get overlooked when the team is busy. When someone raises a concern, they take it seriously, explain what they’ll do and follow through. That’s how trust is built over time.

Leadership potential often shows up long before someone becomes a manager. It’s visible in the support worker who admits a mistake early, helps a colleague build confidence or asks why the same problem keeps happening. Those everyday behaviours give us something concrete to recognise and develop.

Developing leaders

Spotting Tomorrow's Care Leaders

Great Registered Managers rarely start out as managers. They start as support workers who show the right qualities early. We look for those qualities, recognise them and help people build on them.

What makes a good leader in care

Puts people first

Every decision starts with the person being supported — not the rota, not the paperwork.

Stays calm under pressure

When a shift gets hard, they stay steady. That keeps the team steady and the people they support safe.

Owns it

They admit mistakes, learn from them and speak up about concerns. Safeguarding always comes before reputation.

Lifts the team

They notice who is struggling, share what they know and give credit where it is due.

Communicates clearly

They explain things simply to staff, families, social workers and inspectors — and they listen.

Sets the standard

They do the right thing when no one is watching, and others follow their lead.

How we spot it in up-and-coming staff

Colleagues go to them first

When someone is unsure, they ask this person. That trust is the first sign of a leader.

They ask why

They want to understand the reasons behind a care plan or policy, not just follow it.

They step up on a hard shift

They cover gaps and support newer staff without being asked.

Their records are clear

Accurate, timely notes show the care and organisation good leaders need.

They raise concerns

Even when it is uncomfortable, they speak up for the people they support.

They act on feedback

They take advice well and you can see them improve.

Once we spot that potential, we build on it with leadership training, mentoring and a clear path from support worker to senior, deputy and Registered Manager.

    Three settings, three different days. Tap a card to see a typical shift, the skills that transfer and the training you'll get.

    Adult Supported Living

    Helping adults with learning disabilities, autism or mental health needs live in their own homes, their way.

    See a typical shift
    Who you support
    Adults living in their own flat or a shared house, with the support written in their plan.
    A typical shift
    Cooking and shopping together, appointments, medication support, community trips and building independence.
    Skills that transfer
    Patience, cooking, driving, retail or hospitality, caring for family.
    Training
    Induction, safeguarding, medication and the Care Certificate.

    Adult Care Homes

    Caring for older adults and adults with complex needs in a residential home.

    See a typical shift
    Who you support
    Residents who need help with daily living, often including people living with dementia.
    A typical shift
    Personal care, mealtimes, activities, company and conversation, and keeping records up to date.
    Skills that transfer
    Kindness, good listening, teamwork, customer service.
    Training
    Induction, moving and handling, safeguarding and the Care Certificate.

    Children's Residential Care

    Giving children and young people a safe, stable and caring home.

    See a typical shift
    Who you support
    Children and young people living in a children's home, regulated by Ofsted.
    A typical shift
    School runs, homework, meals, activities, keeping children safe and following each child's care plan.
    Skills that transfer
    Youth work, coaching, teaching support, parenting, staying calm under pressure.
    Training
    Induction, safeguarding, therapeutic approaches, and the Level 3 Diploma in Residential Childcare.
